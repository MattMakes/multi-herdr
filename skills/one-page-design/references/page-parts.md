# Page parts

The parts a page can use, and when each one fits. This is a menu, not a layout. Every page gets its own layout, shaped by its design.

## Parts every page has

- **Title**, large, top-left. It states the point.
- **Date**, bottom-right, with an optional status word such as Draft or Proposal.
- **One picture** in the centre.
- **Callouts** in the margins, linked to the picture by thin leader lines or by matching labels. A label may mix digits and letters (`2b`), or give a numeric range (`4-7`).
- **Legend**, whenever a symbol is used more than once.
- **Insets**, optional and small: a thumbnail of the whole world, a floor plan beside a 3D view, a small overview map.

## Picture form by kind of design

| What the reader asks | What to draw |
|---|---|
| Where is everything in this space or level? | A plan from above, or isometric tiles and blocks |
| What does this object or building look like? | The object itself, with a small plan in a corner |
| Where does the trip go, and when? | A map with numbered stops and a ruler of elapsed time |
| What happens, in order, at which spot? | The world, with a numbered circle at each moment |
| How do these similar items differ? | Cards with one shared layout, each with a small picture |
| Which pairs of two attributes exist? | A matrix filled from the top, with an x in each empty cell |
| How do these items act on each other? | A triangle with one axis on each edge, a ring, or a line |
| What happens frame by frame? | A storyboard strip drawn with one set of marks |
| How does one screen or menu lead to the next? (only this) | A flowchart with a small screen thumbnail in each box |

Most pages use two forms together, for example a map with a storyboard strip along the bottom. When no form fits on one surface, the design is probably too complex for one page.

Examples:
- A convoy crosses a frozen lake in 30 minutes of play, with four checkpoints: a map of the lake with numbered stops 1 to 4 and a minute ruler along the bottom edge.
- Weapon types against armour classes, with some pairs unused: a matrix, weapons down the side, armour across the top, an x in each unused cell.
- Title screen, then save-slot picker, then options: a flowchart whose boxes hold small screen thumbnails. This is the only case for a flowchart.

## Cards

A card is a box with rounded corners:

1. A heading with a number and a name.
2. A coloured second line: level, duration, tier or similar.
3. Rows, each with an icon, a name and a short note.

Design the card once and repeat it. Every card on the page has the same layout.

## Marks

Choose a very small fixed set of marks and use only those. For example:

- `O` the player, `X` an enemy.
- A starburst for a hit.
- A blue area for an area effect.
- A triangle for a projectile.
- A coloured dot for a token.

The legend lists the set. Marks replace prose and stay legible from a distance. A page uses one set of marks.

## Time and difficulty

Make them graphics, never sentences:

- A ruler along one edge, in minutes or turns.
- Vertical dividers between phases.
- Shaded difficulty areas on the map.
- Levels stacked down the page to show depth.

## Relationships

- A **triangle** with an axis on each edge turns qualities such as fast or armoured into positions, and turns a simple counter cycle into continuous trade-offs.
- A **matrix** shows where the gaps are.
- A **ring** tells the reader that neighbours are related. Use a ring only when that is true. Six guilds with no neighbour relationships drawn around a circle mislead the reader: refuse that shape and use a matrix or cards instead.

## Text styles

- Story and flavour text: italic.
- Rule text: upright.
- Parameters: short lists in small italic type.
- Formulas: monospace, inside a box.
- A procedure: numbered circles, each with one sentence and a drawing below it.

## Colour

Pale fills with dark outlines, like a printed table mat. One accent colour marks the subject of the page; everything else is grey. When categories matter most, give each category one colour and explain them in the legend.

## Space for reviewers

When people will review the page, leave some areas empty on purpose. Reviewers fill them with pen: they ring a part, strike one out, or add a short note in red.
