# Principles of good reference pages

Read the entry that matches your design before you lay out a page. Each entry pairs a kind of design with a page approach that suits it. The examples are invented for this guide.

The method comes from Stone Librande. For more depth, watch Librande's conference talk on one-page designs.

## A pretty shape is not a true shape

A symmetric shape that looks good, such as a circle, can suggest relationships that do not exist. Arranging eight trade guilds around a ring tells the reader each guild borders two others; if the guilds have no such neighbours, the ring is wrong however good it looks. A shape must not imply neighbours the design does not have.

## Early and rough

A design in an early, rough state needs only a loose chain of isometric areas, each with a name and a short list under it. That is good enough. The page expects reviewers to mark it up by hand, so leave room for their notes.

Example: five floating islands joined by rope bridges, each island a few blocks with its name and three bullet points of what happens there.

## The same design, mature

Once the design settles, it becomes a poster: the map in the centre, cards with one shared layout around the edges, and a leader line from each card to its place on the map.

## A large world

A poster at plotter size: the map in the centre, close-up views around the edges linked to their places by leader lines, a legend near the bottom, and a title block that includes the date.

## A mission or quest

- A small thumbnail of the whole world in the top-left corner, showing where the mission happens.
- The title, with one italic story line under it.
- A central map with locations marked by numbers and letters, each with a short list of what it holds.
- The acts and their goals in a column on one side.
- A legend in one bottom corner and the date in the other.

Example: a rescue mission in a flooded city. Thumbnail of the region top-left; title "Get the pumping-station crew out before the second surge" with an italic line about the rising water; the city streets in the centre with rooftops marked A to F and boats numbered 1 to 3; acts 1 to 3 down the right side; legend bottom-left; date bottom-right.

## Depth or progression

Levels go down the page from top to bottom. Story material sits on one side, grouped by brackets. The other side has one line of specification for each level.

Example: a mine with nine depths, surface at the top; the left side brackets depths 1 to 3, 4 to 6 and 7 to 9 with the story beat of each band; the right side gives each depth's ore, hazard and light level in one line.

## Space and time together

Space goes on the map: numbered paths, and circles for difficulty zones. Time goes on an axis: phase dividers, and a ruler in minutes along the bottom.

## A building with upgrades

One page holds a 3D view in the centre, a labelled floor plan in a corner, the upgrade paths as rows of icons, and a short numbered list of rules.

## Modules that connect

The play area in the centre. On one side, the screen flow as boxes with thumbnails. On the other side, player actions that lead to boxed results. Variants as cards along the bottom.

## A family of related mechanics

A grid of boxed cards. Each card has a name, one sentence, a small diagram that uses the shared set of marks, and an italic list of parameters. Define the set of marks once and use it on every card.

Example: twelve weather spells, each card showing O for the caster, a blue area for the effect and arrows for wind.

## Roles that trade off

A triangle whose edges are the axes. Each region holds a single unit group, drawn as a figure next to a card that lists its traits. The shape shows continuous trade-offs, not a simple three-way counter cycle.

## Two categories crossed

A plain grid filled from the top, with an x in each empty cell.

## One small feature

The page can be letter size and mostly text, with one short storyboard of a few timed frames that uses the shared set of marks.

Example: a dodge roll, in four frames at 0.0, 0.1, 0.25 and 0.4 s.

## A proposal that is mostly calculation

Letter size. Numbered circled steps, each with one sentence and a drawing. Formulas in monospace boxes. Space for a handwritten note that highlights a result. A footer with the date.
