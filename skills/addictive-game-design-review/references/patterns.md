# The 6 patterns

The basic danger of the genre is a solved loop: buy the obvious next thing, wait for the next price, repeat. Games
that last put a new source of decisions in front of the player before the old source is used up. This page gives the
6 patterns that do it, how 6 known games use them, and how to select a pattern for the game you review.

All game descriptions are grade `D` (`evidence.md`): the mechanic exists in a released game. It worked there, at that
pace, with that theme. Take the reason for the mechanic, not its content. Never propose a copy of the names,
characters, art or levels of a game.

## 1. The patterns

| # | Pattern | What it is | The decision it gives | The sign that it is missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P-1 | Constrained builds | More options exist than the player can use. Limited slots force a specialization | "Which of these do I give up?" | The player buys everything in time. The only question is the order |
| P-2 | Bounded randomness | Weighted draws change the offered options. Rerolls, bans and synergies reward knowledge | "What do I make of this offer?" | Each session has the same sequence of purchases |
| P-3 | Production bottlenecks | Conversion, collection or delivery caps the output. The player must diagnose | "What limits me now?" | Output is 1 product of multipliers. Each purchase always helps |
| P-4 | Engineered combinations | 2 independent effects interact and give a result of a different kind | "What do I put together?" | Each upgrade has the same value whatever else the player owns |
| P-5 | Automation as mastery | An old chore becomes a system to configure or automate. The player moves to harder decisions | "When and how does this occur?" | The player does at hour 3 the same input as at minute 3 |
| P-6 | The game changes | Progress shows new interfaces, goals and rules. The objective becomes larger and surprises | "What is this game now?" | The last screen of the game is the first screen with larger numbers |

## 2. The unifying rule for upgrades

A powerful upgrade does 1 or more of these 3 things:

1. It changes the behaviour of a unit.
2. It makes a new interaction.
3. It removes an old constraint.

An upgrade that is only +X% supports the pacing. It must not be the whole design.

The developer of game G-2 below gave a stricter rule in a 2024 interview: the ideal upgrade changes the behaviour of a
unit, is not mandatory, and can combine with other choices to make something new possible.

## 3. Six games

Each game answers the same problem in a different way. Read the row "what stops the stale loop" first.

### G-1 Vampire Survivors: drafted builds in a run

- A character with a start weapon biases the run before the first upgrade.
- 6 weapon slots and 6 passive slots in the normal case. To take 1 item is to close off another.
- A level-up offers 3 or 4 different eligible options from a weighted pool. Rarity weights the draw.
- Evolutions: a weapon at full level, plus a compatible passive item, gives a transformed weapon. The player works
  toward a combination across several uncertain drafts.
- Draft control that the player earns: reroll, banish (remove an option for the run), skip, and seal (exclude an item
  before the run).
- Modifiers that change how a whole build operates, not the level of 1 weapon.
- Meta progress between runs: permanent power-ups, new characters, new stages.
- **What stops the stale loop:** constraints, random offers and synergies make each sequence of choices different, and
  the basic action stays familiar.
- **Take from it:** drafts of 3 at milestones, limited slots, rerolls, exclusions, and combinations to complete.

### G-2 (the) Gnorp Apologue: a visible pipeline

- Damage makes shards at a rock. To collect them is a different operation. As the pile grows, the economy gets a
  bottleneck.
- A high generation rate does not give a high collection rate. Separate unit types do separate work, so the stage
  that limits the output moves with time.
- Specialized workers behave visibly on screen, with different physical results.
- A reset gives talent points. The player activates a subset of talents that change behaviour. More points open more
  groups.
- A scarce special resource pays for the strongest technologies. Where the player spends it selects a strategy.
- A threshold of the pile causes an event that changes the value and the arrangement of the resource.
- **What stops the stale loop:** the player diagnoses a bottleneck that moves, then puts behaviours together that
  change the shape of the production system.
- **Take from it:** separate the stages of production. Let new behaviours change throughput, not add percentages.

### G-3 Antimatter Dimensions: automated mastery

- Tiered producers: each higher tier produces the tier below it. A chain, not 1 generator.
- Nested reset layers. Each resets a different amount of state, gives a new currency, and gives systems that change
  later progress.
- A study tree with branches that exclude each other, and a respec.
- Challenges: changed rules and restrictions as tests of mastery. Their rewards open or improve automation.
- Autobuyers replace manual purchases and resets step by step. The problem changes from "do it" to "configure when
  and how it occurs".
- A late script system runs whole workflows that the player once did by hand.
- A late layer adds randomized equipment with limited slots: a build inside a mostly deterministic game.
- Permanent convenience nodes make each later loop easier and different in structure.
- **What stops the stale loop:** the challenge of yesterday is the automatic baseline of today.
- **Take from it:** automate each mastered chore. Let the player reconfigure branches without a penalty for the
  experiment.

### G-4 Cookie Clicker: engineered moments

- The early game is a simple economy of buildings with rising costs: many cheap units, or save for a strong unit, or
  an efficiency upgrade.
- Random temporary bonuses (for example x7 production, or x777 on clicks) can overlap and give a much larger output
  for a short time.
- A spell system with a resource that regenerates. A spell can fail. 1 spell makes another bonus opportunity, so the
  timing of bonuses is a strategy.
- A loadout of 3 slots of different strength for a subset of spirits, each with a bonus and a drawback.
- A garden with mutation rules to discover, a market with prices that move, and a late research step that changes the
  event economy.
- Persistent upgrades through resets, with a limited number of slots to carry things forward.
- **Key distinction:** the powerful moment is not the random bonus alone. It is the ability of the player to arrange
  a combination of bonuses and to use it when it comes.
- **What stops the stale loop:** the player gets expertise across layered subsystems: production, minigames, loadouts
  and timing.
- **Take from it:** temporary effects that interact, policy slots, and combinations to discover.

### G-5 Universal Paperclips: the question changes

- Price against demand: the price trades profit per sale for demand. Marketing and materials compete for money.
- Automatic makers replace manual production.
- A scarce point resource is assigned between 2 capacities: throughput and storage.
- Several conditional resources, each from a different activity, pay for projects.
- The market interface gives way to infrastructure: factories, harvesters, drones and power limits.
- Late: a budget of finite points across 6 or more attributes of a probe, then hazards that replication alone cannot
  solve.
- The creator describes a journey of 4 to 6 hours from 1 unit to cosmic scale. It ends.
- **What stops the stale loop:** the game replaces the optimization problem itself: a market, then computation, then
  logistics, then exploration.
- **Take from it:** phases, each with its own constraint. A finite arc is a valid design.

### G-6 A Dark Room: discovery as progress

- The player starts with few actions. Interface elements, workers, jobs, crafting and a world map arrive after
  triggers. A new screen is often the prize for mastery of the previous screen.
- Housing raises the population. The player assigns workers to jobs, which makes material dependencies.
- Resources combine into equipment that supports other systems. They do not only raise production.
- 1 item opens a different mode: exploration of a map.
- Water, food, capacity and equipment limit how far an expedition goes.
- **What stops the stale loop:** discovery changes the scope that the player perceives. Resources become tools for
  exploration, and exploration opens the next mystery.
- **Take from it:** show the next thing only when the player has a reason to use it.

## 4. What each game uses

| Game | Choice structure | Main source of variation | Carry-over | Its own reward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G-1 | Limited equipment and a weighted draft | Random offers, characters, modifiers, stages | Strong, between runs | A completed evolved build |
| G-2 | Talent allocation and a scarce resource | Specializations and bottlenecks that move | Talents from a reset | Combined behaviour that the player can see |
| G-3 | Study paths, challenges, equipment loadouts | New reset layers, late random equipment | Very strong, in layers | The automation of earlier work |
| G-4 | Production investment and configurable subgames | Temporary effects, minigames, timing | Resets with persistent benefits | An engineered combination of multipliers |
| G-5 | Scarce points and investment in systems | Phase objectives that change | Mostly 1 campaign | A sudden expansion of scale |
| G-6 | Worker jobs, crafting, exploration | Systems and events that appear | Story progress | Gameplay that the player did not expect |

Combinations that fit together:

- **Foundation:** G-2 with G-5. A physical production chain with bottlenecks that move, which later grows into larger
  management problems.
- **Build variety:** G-1 with G-4. Technologies in limited slots, bounded drafts, combinations to discover, and
  opportunities that the player learns to engineer.
- **Mastery:** G-3. Automation replaces each recurring chore, and control grows with competence.
- **Presentation:** G-6 with G-2. Systems appear when they become relevant, and each investment shows in the world.

## 5. Select a pattern for the game you review

Do not add a pattern because a known game has it. Select by the fault and by the promise of the game.

| The fault you found | First pattern to try | The smallest version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The player buys everything; only the order changes (R-1, R-3) | P-1 | 1 set of slots: N things equipped from more than N owned, with a free respec |
| Each purchase always helps; no diagnosis (R-4) | P-3 | Split 1 multiplier into 2 stages with separate upgrades, and show the stage that limits |
| Upgrades are almost all +X% (R-2, R-5) | P-4 | Replace 3 number upgrades with 3 pair effects, each visible on screen |
| Each session is the same sequence (R-6) | P-2 | A draft of 3 at each milestone, for optional technologies only, with 1 reroll |
| The same input at hour 3 as at minute 3 (R-7) | P-5 | An automation for the oldest chore, bought with the resource that the chore makes |
| The last screen is the first screen with larger numbers (R-8) | P-6 | 1 phase change: a new constraint and a new goal at about the middle of the content |

Rules of fit:

1. **The pace of the game.** A game played in active sessions with no offline gain needs decisions per minute.
   Patterns P-1, P-3 and P-4 fit. A game with long waits needs P-5 early.
2. **Permanence.** A fantasy of permanent restoration or growth conflicts with hard resets. Use builds that the
   player reconfigures (P-1 with a respec), not runs that erase. A reset is correct only when the fantasy carries it.
3. **Linear is a baseline, not a design.** A game in which each technology opens in a fixed order is simple to build
   and to pace. It has few distinct builds, and the player solves it. Keep the linear core for essentials, and put
   the choices beside it.
4. **1 pattern at a time.** Propose the pattern that opens the worst fault. Say which pattern comes second, and why
   it waits. 6 patterns in 1 review is a new game, not a review.
5. **Randomness needs guarantees.** With P-2, what the player needs to operate stays for sale at all times. A bad
   draft must never block the player. Early drafts are broad. Later the player earns rerolls, exclusions and
   preferences.

## 6. What a good upgrade does to a chain

Production and useful output are not the same operation. When a game has separate stages, useful output per second is
the smallest of the stage capacities:

```
useful output = min(stage 1 rate, stage 2 capacity, stage 3 capacity)
```

A game where the smallest stage is stage 3 gains nothing from more of stage 1. That is the decision: the player must
diagnose, not multiply.

A good upgrade has a second-order result. Find it for each upgrade of the game:

| Upgrade type | Direct effect | Second-order result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More endurance of a producer | More sustained stage 1 output | Shows the stage 2 bottleneck |
| Better conversion | Better efficiency of stage 2 | Makes more producers valuable again |
| Storage | Holds the excess | Makes short surges of stage 1 valuable |
| Better delivery or routing | More stage 3 capacity | Turns wasted output into visible progress |
| Automatic care of a producer | Less downtime | Raises the importance of stage 2 capacity |

Storage is not a fourth number in the `min()` chain. It has a capacity (an amount), charge and discharge rates, and a
level. It moves a surplus across a peak of demand. Model it as its own system.

Automation is a promotion, in steps:

```
manual chore -> local automation -> policies for 1 place -> strategy across places
```

When a player has shown that they understand a task, the same clicks reduce enjoyment. Replace mastered work with a
decision of a higher order. An upgrade should let the player stop an action, not only do more of it.
